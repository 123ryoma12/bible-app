For the Israelites, people, objects, places, and even time could be placed along a spectrum with holiness on one side and uncleanness on the other. Holiness is associated with God, for He alone is inherently holy. At the other extreme is uncleanness, associated with forces opposed to God. To be in God’s presence requires a person to be holy; sin causes him to be ritually unclean and means that he cannot come near God. Anyone who is unclean or who has come in contact with uncleanness is to abstain from public worship until he is cleansed. Because holiness is linked to God, it is associated with perfection and life. Uncleanness, in marked contrast, is linked to chaos and death. Normal, healthy creatures are clean, but abnormal or unhealthy creatures, and particularly corpses, are unclean. Abnormalities that suggest or that might lead to death are marks of uncleanness. Life and death are not to be mixed, so the unclean is never to enter God’s presence. Ultimately, it is Christ, the great Mediator and High Priest, who triumphs over death and corruption in behalf of those who trust in Him (1 Cor. 15:20–28, 50–57).\n\n**11:1–47** Moses instructs the Israelites regarding the principles of cleanness, showing them that, though all corpses are unclean (vv. 24, 27, 31–40), some perfectly healthy creatures are also stated to be unclean in a milder sense and therefore must not be eaten. Although some of the forbidden animals posed a possible health threat (e.g., pigs as carriers of trichinosis), the classification of uncleanness is not based on health grounds. Rather, the principle teaches basic moral and spiritual truths. Carnivorous animals and birds of prey eat flesh with blood in it, something forbidden to human beings (7:26). Such animals also come into contact with carcasses, which are unclean. The clean creatures here symbolize Israelites, while unclean animals symbolize Gentiles. Only domesticated clean animals may be offered in sacrifice, for the sacrificial animal symbolizes the priest. In restricting his diet to clean animals, the Israelite was reminded that God had chosen Israel alone among the nations. It is associated with wholeness and perfection. Only God is intrinsically holy. He is by nature majestic, awesome, pure, and perfect. He demonstrated His transcendent holiness by His creation of the world (see Gen. 1:1, 3 and notes), by His great acts in history such as redeeming His people from Egypt, and by His perfect laws. Israel’s holiness is derived from her unique covenant relationship with the only true and living God. His covenant provides, among other things, moral and judicial laws that reflect God’s own righteous standards, and the sacrificial system to atone for sin. The immediate background for this designation of Israel as a “holy nation” is the Lord’s appearance on Mount Sinai: they washed their clothes, avoided contact with the mountain, and abstained from sexual intercourse in preparation for that unique event (Ex. 19:10–15, 21–24; Heb. 12:18–21).
