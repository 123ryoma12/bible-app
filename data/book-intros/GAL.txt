Paul names his addressees as “Galatians” (3:1; cf. 1:2), but to which “Galatians” was he writing? Galatia was a Roman province that encompassed roughly the eastern portion of Asia Minor (modern-day Turkey). He may have been writing specifically to the Celtic people who lived in northern Galatia and who were widely known as “Galatians,” or he might have been addressing the people who lived in southern Galatia in central Asia Minor. When we follow the course of Paul’s first and second missionary journeys (Acts 13; 14; 15:36–18:22), we discover that this question has implications for the epistle’s date and for its relationship to Paul’s other letters. Paul visited Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe (all cities in southern Galatia) on his first and second missionary journeys. If Paul wrote to southern Galatia, he probably wrote to those churches early in his career, shortly after the first missionary journey, or about the time of the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15; cf. Gal. 2:11–14). The date most often given by those who hold this view is A.D. 49. If this is correct, Galatians may be Paul’s earliest epistle in existence today.","Many scholars, however, think that Galatians was written to the ethnic Galatians in the north. If this view is correct, Paul may have planted the Galatian churches on his second missionary journey (Acts 16:6), and probably wrote the letter after passing through “Galatia and Phrygia” (Acts 18:23) on his third missionary journey. Many who follow the “northern Galatian theory” believe that Paul wrote the letter either during his two-year stay in Ephesus (Acts 19) or as he was traveling through Macedonia on his way to Greece at the end of his third missionary journey (Acts 20:1–6; cf. 2 Cor. 2:13). If this is correct, Galatians was probably written in A.D. 54 or 55.","Theories that date the letter later in Paul’s career have the merit of placing Galatians in the same period as 2 Corinthians, Romans, and perhaps Philippians—letters that share with Galatians some matters of common concern. The earlier (southern Galatian) dating, on the other hand, is more easily reconciled with the account of Paul’s movements in the book of Acts. In Galatians, he reports two visits to Jerusalem, the first coming three years after his conversion (1:18–20; Acts 9:26–30) and the second “after fourteen years” (perhaps from his conversion, rather than from the first visit; 2:1–10; Acts 11:29, 30). In order to understand the epistle, some knowledge of the situation that provoked Paul to write is essential. Not long after the Galatians had accepted the gospel, agitators came among them who attacked Paul personally (4:17) and preached a distorted form of Christianity, a false gospel (1:6, 7). Their “gospel” required circumcision for justification (6:12). Since the Galatians were uncircumcised Gentiles, the agitators insisted that the Galatians not only believe in Christ for justification but also accept circumcision (2:3–5; 5:2, 6, 11; 6:12, 13, 15).","The zeal of these agitators likely reflects Jewish pressure as well as their own pride. Perhaps they were attempting to convert the Gentile Galatians to Judaism under pressure from nationalistic Jewish groups in Judea who, according to the Jewish historian Josephus, were becoming increasingly intolerant of contact between Jews and Gentiles during the last half of the first century.","The agitators were not content merely to preach their brand of the gospel. Their attacks took three forms: (1) They claimed that Paul was a renegade who had defied his superiors, the Jerusalem apostles. Paul responds to this attack in 1:11–2:10. (2) They said that Paul had recently argued with Peter over whether the gospel required Gentiles to become Jews in order to become Christians. Paul gives his account of the encounter with Peter in 2:11–14. (3) The agitators spread the notion that Paul had originally preached circumcision for salvation (5:11) but had recently changed his gospel so that he might more easily accommodate the Gentiles (1:10).","The Galatians, for their part, were showing interest both in the rumors about Paul and in the agitators’ new form of the gospel. They now wanted to be “under the law” (4:21; cf. 5:1) and, specifically, to become circumcised (5:2). This attraction to “a different gospel” (1:6) bore bitter fruit. It contains a standard letter opening (1:1–5), body (1:6–6:10), and conclusion (6:11–18). It is important to note, however, that while Paul uses the standard conventions of first-century letter writing, he does not do so slavishly. He feels free to adapt the form when it suits his specific needs. For example, in his letter to the Galatians, he does not include a thanksgiving immediately after his opening salutation, as he does in his other letters. Instead, he moves immediately into a rebuke of the Galatian believers. In 1:11–2:14, Paul gives an extended historical narrative (the narratio), the goal of which is to defend the authenticity of his apostleship and to respond to criticisms that it had originated from men. In 2:15–21, Paul presents the thesis, or main proposition (the propositio), of the letter. The argument defending that proposition follows in 3:1–4:11 (the argumentum). After an expression of personal concern (4:12–20) and an illustration drawn from the biblical account of Hagar and Sarah (4:21–31), Paul proceeds to exhort the Galatians to holy living in Gal 5:1–6:10 (the exhortatio). Galatians lacks an opening prayer of thanksgiving, as well as closing greetings and prayer requests. The Protestant Reformers drew a parallel between this view among first-century Jews in Paul’s day and later medieval Roman Catholicism in the sixteenth century because of the emphasis in both of those groups on the role of the believer’s good works in meriting a right standing before the Lord. Justification, according to the New Perspective, is not about how one gets saved, but rather about how one identifies the church.
