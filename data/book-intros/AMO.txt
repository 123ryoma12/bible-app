As with almost all poetical literature of the Bible, the written materi­al takes on a variety of forms: so in Amos we see oracles related poetically (1:3–2:16); hymns given poetically (4:13; 5:8, 9; 9:5, 6), and sermons presented poetically (3:1–6:14). The prophet is a master of striking imagery. Just consider 7:1–9:10, wherein Amos presents five pictures that demonstrate graphically the coming judgment of God. These symbols add a level of vividness to the prophet’s message that prose narrative cannot convey.","The message of Amos may be divided into four major sections. The first two chapters are composed of proclamations of God’s judgment against Israel and seven of her neighbors: Damascus, Philistia, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab, and Judah (1:3–2:16). All the neighboring nations are accused of crimes against humanity, such as physical violence and oppression against other people. The prophet brings the longest accusation against Israel (2:6–16).","The second section deals more specifically with the sins of the northern kingdom of Israel, and the divine judgment that will come upon that nation is expanded and elucidated (3:1–6:14). Amos confronts the rulers of Israel with their wicked and unjust ways, and he calls them to repentance. The coming judgment he proclaims includes the destruction of palaces and holy places, the overthrow of the kingdom, and exile for the people (5:27).","The third section consists of five visions of the prophet (7:1–9:10). The first two visions (locusts and flood, 7:1–6) threaten divine judgment against Israel. The next two visions of a plumb line (7:7–9) and a basket of ripe fruit (8:1–14) point out the reality that Israel cannot avert God’s sentence. The final vision of God in the temple (9:1–10) pictures Him poised to strike in judgment.","The last section of the prophecy promises a restoration of the fallen people of Israel (9:11–15). Up to this point the prophet has homed in on the certainty of judgment that is to come, but now he closes with great hope for a post-judgment restoration of the house of David. It was essential that these great covenant lawsuit messages be preserved both as reminders of Israel’s history and for the promises of restoration and redemption they contained.","Amos attacks two major areas of sin commonly indicted by the prophets: idolatry and social injustice.
