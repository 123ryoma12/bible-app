The prophet has in view a military assault on Jerusalem in which the Edomites gleefully took part (vv. 11–14), but he does not provide information that clearly dates the catastrophe. Some link the book to an invasion of Judah by Philistines and Arabs during King Jehoram’s reign (848–841 B.C..), in which it is presumed that the Edomites participated (2 Kin. 8:20–22; 2 Chr. 21:8–10, 16, 17). Others relate the events prompting this prophecy to the Babylonian invasions of Judah, which resulted eventually in her collapse in 586 B.C.. Both Scripture (Ps. 137; Ezek. 35:1–15). Jewish tradition explicitly mentions the Edomites’ involvement in this final catastrophe, and the text of Obadiah seems to refer more naturally to the Bab­ylonian assaults. Furthermore, striking similarities between vv. 1–9 and Jer. 49:7–9, 14–16 suggest that Obadiah and Jeremiah were addressing Edom at roughly the same time. While we cannot date Obadiah precisely, we know that Jeremiah prophesied from about 626 B.C.. until after 586 B.C.. Irony, striking imagery, double entendre, and deliberate use of repetition are all employed to good effect. This is immediately followed by words addressed to Edom that set out God’s case against this nation and the nature of her punishment (vv. 2–18). In the final verses of the book, God describes the restoration of exiled Israel and the establishment of God’s kingdom (vv. 19–21). Edom prospered, Judah lay defeated, and the moral order of the world appeared to have been overthrown by lawless forces. But the prophet Obadiah was raised up with a message of God’s sovereign justice in order to strengthen His people’s weakened faith.
