These critics said that the regulation of the central sanctuary (ch. 12) was invented by a writer who supported Josiah’s reform. In more recent years, however, this view has received less favor. Some have dated Deuteronomy as early as Samuel, others as late as the exile. Many critics still date the book in the seventh century B.C., to the period of Josiah. But these scholars also question the unity of the book. If some parts seem “early” (from Moses’s time), they ascribe those parts to an old tradition that has conveniently been preserved. If other parts seem “late” (during or after the time of Josiah), they are said to be “later editions” or to result from “late editing.” However, there is no textual support for these elastic, subjective, and speculative conclusions. No concrete evidence rules out the composition of Deuteronomy at the time of Moses, making reasonable allowances for additions by someone who appended Moses’s obituary to the book, and for some later updating of Hebrew grammar and place names.","The background and setting of the book reflect conditions prior to the conquest of Canaan under Joshua. There is no mention of a king in Judah or of the city of Jerusalem, which is named over one hundred times by the prophet Jeremiah (who wrote in Josiah’s day). It is unlikely that a sixth-century author would make no allusion whatever to that capital city or its temple. The twelve tribes are represented as one nation (instead of, as in Josiah’s period, the kingdoms of Judah and Israel). The Transjordan cities of refuge are named, while those in Canaan (which are named later by Joshua) are not. The Babylonian names of months are not used, and there are no Persian loan words in the vocabulary, even though such words would be expected to appear in a work supposedly written during a period when these empires were dominant. Moses, Aaron, and Joshua are named, but no later persons or later historical incidents are mentioned. It is unlikely that a later author, even one well-versed in the lore of the past, could so completely avoid the use of later terms and the mention of persons and events of his own period.","Some scholars have noted the general conformity of Deuteronomy’s structure to the covenant or treaty form of the mid-second millennium B.C. (the approximate time of Moses). They find the following treaty elements in Deuteronomy: (a) a preamble identifying the covenant mediator (1:1–5); (b) a historical prologue reviewing previous covenant history (1:6–4:40); (c) stipulations expounding the covenant way of life (4:44–11:32; 12–26); (d) a declaration of sanctions stating the blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience of the covenant (chs. 27–30); and (e) a provision for the administration of the covenant after the death of the inaugural mediator (chs. 31–34). Though aspects of this approach have much merit, the present layout of Deuteronomy is shaped by two lengthy exhortatory speeches by Moses. While these speeches possibly mirror in part the original form of the covenant initiated at Mount Sinai, the present text of Deuteronomy incorporates that form within the overall narrative of the Pentateuch. With the conquest of Transjordan successfully concluded, Moses challenged the Israelites in these farewell addresses. Deuteronomy combines exhortation and instructions, and it serves as an example of how the covenant obligations should be taught.","The opening address (1:5–4:40) recounts the experiences of Israel under Moses’s leadership. Deuteronomy does not speak of how Moses confronted Pharaoh and how the miraculous signs and wonders forced Pharaoh to let the people go, but it alludes to the exodus repeatedly (five times in the first address: 1:20, 34; 4:20, 34, 37). Moses recounts God’s providential and miraculous care for the people during the journey from Egypt to Sinai. Then he details their defeat both spiritually and militarily at Kadesh-barnea. There are references here to events recorded in Numbers, but like the record in Numbers, almost nothing is said about events during the forty years of wilderness wandering. The journey around Edom toward Transjordan is mentioned, and the defeat of the kings Sihon and Og is recorded in fuller detail than in Numbers. Then comes the allocation of land in Transjordan for the tribes of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh (as in Num. 32), and the narrative ends with reference to Moses’s plea for himself to enter Canaan, which God disallows (as in Num. 27:12–23). Moses concludes the first speech with exhortations to be loyal to the Lord.","The second speech begins with exhortations (5:1–11:32). The address continues to 26:19, with Moses’s expounding the statutes and judgments linked to the Sinai covenant (chs. 12–26). The speech begins with the Ten Commandments, almost exactly like the wording in Ex. 20, aside from the fourth commandment (5:12–15 note). The terror of the theophany (a visible self-revelation of God) at Mount Sinai is recalled, with a summons by Moses to obedience. At Mount Sinai, only the Ten Commandments were given directly by the voice of God; the rest of the covenant obligations were mediated through Moses (5:22 note). The famous Shema, “Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one,” is given in 6:4, with the exhortation to teach, remember, and obey. Chapters 6–11 are sprinkled with examples of God’s care and judgments since leaving Egypt—all allusions to material in Exodus and Numbers. These examples serve to warn Israel to trust the Lord and not themselves. This leads to a promise of success in the coming conflict in Canaan.","The statutes and judgments in chs. 12–26 include regulations for worship, clean foods, slaves and debts, annual feasts, judges, cities of refuge, and various matters of conduct. Most of these have some parallel to material found in the previous books of the Pentateuch, which parallels are noted at the appropriate places.","The final chapters of Deuteronomy contain a mixture of shorter speeches by Moses and narrative reporting. In chs. 27–30 Moses exhorts the Israelites yet again to obey the instructions or Torah that he has given them. Moses gives instructions for a solemn ceremony to be held in the valley between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim, near Shechem, after Israel has secured a foothold in Canaan—a ceremony reminiscent of the covenant ceremony of Ex. 20:1–24:8, and that Joshua later carries out (Josh. 8:30–35).
