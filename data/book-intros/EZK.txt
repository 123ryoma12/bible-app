After the Babylonians defeated the Egyptians at Carchemish in 605 B.C., Jehoiakim switched his allegiance and became a vassal of Nebuchadnezzar. He remained a Babylonian vassal for three years and then transferred his allegiance back to Egypt (2 Kin. 24:1). Jehoiakim died in the same month that Nebuchadnezzar set out on an expedition to punish him. He was succeeded by his son Jehoiachin, who was left to face Nebuchadnezzar’s wrath. Oracles are straightforward pronouncements of judgment or hope for the people, outlining the sins for which judgment is coming upon them (especially in the first part of the book) or the attributes of God that will lead Him to deliver the remnant of His people (in the latter portion of the book).","A subcategory of this genre consists of the oracles against foreign nations, which occur at the turning point of the book in ch. 24 as the news of the fall of Jerusalem comes to the exiles. A sequence of seven oracles against the nations (chs. 25–32) charges them with delighting in Jerusalem’s fall and assisting the Babylonians in their destructive acts. This was good news for Israel in exile, for it showed that at least part of the promise made to Abraham in Gen. 12:3—“him who dishonors you I will curse”—was still intact. Judgment would come on all those who delighted in the downfall of God’s people and profited from their demise.","Sign-acts, in which the prophet accompanies his message with a striking or memorable action that illustrates his point, were particularly prevalent in Ezekiel’s ministry (3:22–26; 4:1–14; 5:1–4; 12:10–20; 21:6, 7, 18–24; 24:15–24; 37:15–28). These were useful means of revelation for resistant audiences who were reluctant to accept God’s word through the prophet. The sign-acts functioned as acted parables, reinforcing the unpalatable message. Ezekiel also used many parables (chs. 15–17; 19; 23) and proverbs (12:21, 22; 16:44; 18:2, 3).","The visions that the prophet received lent authenticity to his message. He had personally encountered the God of whom he spoke, and in visions he had been shown the true state of those left behind in Jerusalem, as well as the future for God’s people after the exile. These visions added texture and memorability to the prophet’s words, and the glorious picture of the future that the visions depicted were an encouragement to God’s people to remain faithful in an often dismal and difficult present. False prophets wrongly encouraged this belief. Since the Lord had chosen Jerusalem as His dwelling and had Himself defended the city in the past (see Is. 36; 37), many people believed Jerusalem was inviolable. In the face of this, Ezekiel proclaimed the still-greater power and glory of the Lord. Revelation to Ezekiel was therefore often mediated by an angelic guide (chs. 8; 40–48). When Ezekiel did see a vision of God, he could only describe it as “the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the Lord” rather than provide precise details. God’s judgment of Judah and Jerusalem does not frustrate His purpose in electing Israel. Ezekiel’s message to the exiles was that God would again be in their midst (48:35; cf. 11:20; 14:11; 36:23, 27, 28). The nation would again live under a Davidic prince (37:24, 25; 45:7) who rules righteously (34:23). God would give to His people a new heart and a new spirit (36:24–28). An ancient Jewish rabbi, Hananiah ben Hezekiah, is reported to have burned three hundred barrels of oil in his lamp before he was able to resolve all of the differences between Ezek­iel and the laws of Moses. Other rabbis considered the book too dangerous to be read by anyone under the age of thirty. Given its complex nature, early interpreters often adopted an allegorical approach to exegesis. In a homily on Ezek. 40:6–8, Gregory the Great identified the east gate of the temple as Jesus, the steps leading up to the gate as the merits of the virtues that lead to salvation, and the threshold of the gate as the ancestors of Jesus. For Thomas Aquinas, the closed gate in Ezek. 44:1–3 was the classic proof text for the perpetual virginity of Mary.","From at least the time of the Reformation, chs. 38; 39 have often been read against the background of current events.
