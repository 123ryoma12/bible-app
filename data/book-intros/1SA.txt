The books do contain information from sources contemporary with the events narrated in them, such as David’s eulogies for Saul and his sons (2 Sam. 1:17–27) and his lament for Abner (2 Sam. 3:33–34). They rely to some degree on stories, chronicles, and poems that came from an earlier period. The text itself gives evidence that demonstrates that its final composition was later than the recorded events. For example, 1 Sam. 27:6 says, “Therefore Ziklag has belonged to the kings of Judah to this day.” That statement denotes that a number of kings have ruled over Judah since the death of Solomon and the division of the monarchy. Found also in that verse is an expression common in the books of Samuel: “to this day” (1 Sam. 5:5; 6:18; 27:6; 30:25; 2 Sam. 4:3; 6:8; 18:18), which indicates that the writer is narrating events that had taken place prior to his time.","It is reasonable to believe that the final editing and composition of the books occurred in the ninth or eighth centuries B.C., that is, sometime after David’s reign. It is likely that an official court historian wrote 1 and 2 Samuel within one to three centuries of the events. There is little compelling reason to think that the books were given a final form in either the exilic or the postexilic period.","Although the exact dates of the events recorded in Samuel are elusive, the general chronology of the period can be defined. Definite figures are given for the duration of David’s reign over Judah and Israel (2 Sam. 5:4–5), but there is no clear indication of how long Saul reigned (1 Sam. 13:1 note). The specific years of David’s and Saul’s reigns have been established by working back from the dates of events recorded in Kings, which have their own chronological difficulties (see Introduction to 1 Kings). There is broad consensus that David began his rule in Hebron around 1010 B.C. and died around 970 B.C. Samuel may have been born about 1100 B.C. Thus, the books of Samuel cover a period that includes most of the eleventh century B.C. and the first quarter of the tenth century B.C.","In the latter half of the eleventh century B.C., a time when international powers of the ancient Near East were preoccupied with internal conflicts, Israel was transformed from a loosely knit tribal confederation into a united monarchy. The institution of the monarchy was a new stage in the political and religious history of Israel, although the idea of kingship itself would have been known to Israel from the practice of its neighbors (Judg. 3:12; 4:2; 8:5). It is remarkable not that Israel eventually installed a king but that it resisted doing so for so long. One cause of Israel’s reluctance is revealed in the words of Gideon: “I will not rule over you, and my son will not rule over you; the Lord will rule over you” (Judg. 8:23). A fundamental tenet of Israel’s faith was that the Lord Himself (Yahweh) was Israel’s sovereign, their great King (1 Sam. 8:7; 12:12; Num. 23:21; Ps. 5:2; Mal. 1:14). Nevertheless, the opening books of the Bible contain indications that Israel, in accordance with the divine will, would one day have a human monarch (Gen. 49:10; Num. 24:7, 17–19; cf. Gen. 17:6, 16; 35:11). Moses anticipated a time when Israel would be settled in the Promised Land and would desire a king, and he gave instructions to regulate kingship when it came (Deut. 17:14–20; cf. 28:36). As 1 Samuel opens, that time has almost arrived.","The two books of Samuel are not a formal, comprehensive history of Israel during the monarchic period. Rather, the biblical writer focuses primarily on the three most prominent Israelite leaders of the time: Samuel, Saul, and David. 1 Samuel 1–8 describes Samuel’s birth, call to ministry, and judgeship. Samuel is often considered the last judge of Israel, and he was a pivotal figure between the era of the judges and the monarchic period. The next section, 1 Samuel 9–31, recounts the failed kingship of Saul, the first ruler of Israel. It relates his being chosen as king, his anointing to the office by the prophet Samuel, his early military victories, his repeated disobedience to the commands of God, his final rejection as king, and his death at the hands of the Philistines during the battle at Mount Gilboa. Second Samuel is a narration of the kingship of David. The founding of a monarchy for Israel was not bad in itself; in fact, its very roots go back to the book of Genesis. Contained in the divine revelations to the patriarchs was a promise of kings coming through their posterity (Gen. 17:6, 16; 35:11). Jacob prophesied that the scepter, the symbol of kingly authority, would not depart from the tribe of Judah until the ultimate, final king would appear (Gen. 49:10). The institution of a monarchy in Israel was clearly in accord with the eternal plan of God, and He declared that truth in the Scriptures prior to Israel’s settlement in Canaan (Deut. 17:14–20).","This kingship, however, was to be different from the monarchies of the nations surrounding Israel. The human ruler of Israel was not to be autonomous; rather, he was to be subject to God and fully dependent on God’s word as the rule and authority of the kingdom. Thus, the foundation of biblical kingship is that ultimately God is king and will always be king. As the psalmist proclaims, “The Lord is king forever and ever” (Ps. 10:16). Under this structure, a theocratic monarchy, Israel was to have a human king who served as deputy under the Lord, the ultimate king.","When Israel sought a king (1 Sam. 8), the elders, moved by a sense of military insecurity, advocated for a king similar to the kings of the nations around them. They wanted a human king who would deliver them from their enemies, which amounted to a rejection of the theocratic monarchy. The Lord Himself assessed Israel’s choice: “They have rejected me from being king over them” (v. 7).","In response, the prophet Samuel warned Israel that their desire for such a king would have dire consequences, such as oppression of the people (1 Sam. 8:10–18), but the people dismissed his argument and demanded a king similar to the other ancient Near Eastern monarchs. In response God selected Saul to be the first king of Israel. The choice of Saul reflects one of the important themes of the books of Samuel: God raises up and brings down (see 1 Sam. 2:7–8).","Unfortunately, Israel’s first king, Saul, turned out to be exactly what Samuel had warned against. Saul’s kingship began well, but it soon became dark and dysfunctional. He was followed on the throne by David, who was the greatest king in the history of old covenant Israel. He was a ruler after God’s own heart, and his kingship started out quite well. However, David soon fell into deep sin, particularly seen in his relationship with Bathsheba, and throughout the second half of 2 Samuel, David’s kingdom was in disarray. By the close of the book, the reader is struck by the flawed and broken nature of David’s kingship.","David’s kingship is properly understood as a pointer to the coming of a greater king, that is, the messianic monarch (2 Sam. 7:12–16). In other words, David himself was not the messianic ruler; he was a forerunner of him. This is evident by the close of 2 Samuel, when David’s kingdom is shown in turmoil and disorder. The reader thus longs for the arrival of the promised king who would live according to God’s word (Deut. 17:14–20). The messianic king eventually came in the person of Jesus Christ, “the son of David” (e.g., Matt. 9:27; 12:23; 21:9).","In 1 and 2 Samuel, Israel is in the process of becoming a kingdom, which can be defined as “a land or realm over which a monarch rules.” Earlier, at Mount Sinai, God vowed that His people would one day be a “kingdom” and a “nation” (Ex. 19:6). From Sinai, God led Israel through the wilderness to the Land of Promise, and they then conquered Canaan under the leadership of Joshua. The subsequent period of the judges was one of anarchy. All of that led to the events recorded in 1 and 2 Samuel. Despite the initially promising reigns of Saul, David, and Solomon, each of these rulers demonstrated an inability to exercise the righteous kingship that God’s people needed. After the death of Solomon, the monarchy divided into the two kingdoms of Israel and Judah. Both of those kingdoms were eventually destroyed and their populations sent into exile.","The failures of the kings of Israel and Judah illustrate the need for a greater ruler.
