In chs. 1–9, there are a number of longer poems that are similar to the “instruction” form of some early Egyptian literature. Some relationship in form and content between Israelite and non-Israelite wisdom should not surprise us given the common concerns of wisdom in all cultures. Whether the instruction form was used in schools for the elite (as in Egypt) or in the home (as was probably the case in Israel), the content is wisdom for life as it was taught to young people by their elders. The instruction generally begins with an address (“my son”) coupled with an initial motivation, followed by a lesson and then a conclusion that may include the consequences of heeding or not heeding the lesson.","Another major form in Proverbs is the short saying, found mainly in 10:1–22:16 and 25:1–29:27. In short, wisdom is the skill to survive and thrive by living in accordance with the order that God has established in his world. For more on these themes, see Introduction to the Wisdom Literature.","There are many other themes in the book of Proverbs, including how to use speech in a way that benefits and does not harm, how to handle one’s sexual drive, how to be industrious and enjoy the benefits of labor, how to avoid sloth and poverty, how to relate well to both God and neighbor, how to love the right woman, and how to think about life and death. While some of these themes are addressed in more extended poems, many of them are addressed in short proverbs scattered throughout the book. So, while there is always benefit in reading the chapters in Proverbs sequentially, there is also benefit in collecting proverbs on a given theme that are scattered throughout the book, making it possible to study all that the book of Proverbs has to say on a particular topic. This is especially helpful in regard to chapters 10–31. As an example, consider a brief look at one theme: beneficial words. Second, beneficial words also benefit oneself. “From the fruit of his mouth a man is satisfied with good, and the work of a man’s hand comes back to him” (12:14); the words one speaks can bring one as much benefit as the work one does. The Law sets forth the foundational principles of the covenant and the ideal life to be experienced by following the principles of the covenant. The Former Prophets show the failure of the covenant people to experience this ideal in history. The Latter Prophets provide divine interpretation of this failure in history. The book of Proverbs is in the third and final section of the Hebrew Bible, the Writings. The overall function of this section is to teach the covenant community how to live out the ideals set forth in the Law but not experienced in the Prophets. One implication of reading the book of Proverbs in this context is to realize that while it does not contain explicit teaching on redemptive history, its position within that history is presumed. Among other things, this means that the principles taught in the book of Proverbs are to be lived out not as legalistic rules but as a response to God’s redemptive work in the past on behalf of the community. For new covenant believers, this means that we strive to live out the principles in the book of Proverbs as our response to God’s grace to us in the redemptive work of Jesus Christ. James is sometimes called the New Testament version of Proverbs. How believers should read James is a good model for how believers should read Proverbs and the other Wisdom Literature books.
