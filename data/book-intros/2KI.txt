It traces the history of the northern kingdom to its destruction at the hands of the Assyrians in 722 B.C. (2 Kings 17:6). After this conquest, Hezekiah of Judah (715–686 B.C.) established a great religious reform in the southern kingdom (18:1–4). His reformation is a high point in the book; however, it did not continue after his death. One of the final eras of hope for Judah was the reign of Josiah (640–609 B.C.). He attempted to free Judah from Assyrian control, to bring about a religious reform in Judah, and to integrate the territory of the former northern kingdom into his own land. After Josiah was killed at Megiddo in 609 B.C., Judah was, for all intents and purposes, finished as a nation. In 586 B.C. the Babylonian army delivered the final blow as it captured Judah and Jerusalem, destroyed many Judean cities, and then sent many of the Judeans into exile. However, the last recorded event in this book is the release of Jehoiachin from prison. His exaltation above all the other exiled kings in Babylon offers a glimmer of hope that God has not abandoned His commitment to establish David’s dynasty forever.
