Origen, in the third century, was the first writer outside of the Bible to attribute the epistle explicitly to Peter, but he said that others doubted Peter’s authorship. Eusebius listed it among the disputed NT books, and Jerome, while noting some disagreement regarding its authenticity, suggested that stylistic differences with 1 Peter were due to Peter’s use of different secretaries. The epistle was accepted as authentic and canonical by influential fourth-century church fathers such as Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Ambrose, and Augustine, as well as by the late-fourth-century church councils of Hippo and Carthage, and its subsequent place in the NT canon was assured.","Notwithstanding the epistle’s own claims, a number of objections have been raised to Petrine authorship. Among the more common ones are the lack of early attestation, slow recognition by the church, stylistic differences with 1 Peter, and the apparent use of Hellenistic religious and philosophical language. The usual alternative to Petrine authorship is pseudonymity, i.e., it was written by an unknown author who attributed the work to a well-known writer as a literary device to commend his message. While several of the objections must be taken seriously (especially attestation and style), their force should not be exaggerated, and none conclusively disproves Peter’s authorship.","On the one hand, there is stylistic diversity between 1 and 2 Peter. Many of the favorite words and expressions of 1 Peter, for example, are absent in 2 Peter. On the other hand, the differences are not absolute, and several striking similarities do exist between the two epistles. There are also a number of parallels between 2 Peter and Peter’s speeches in Acts (e.g., the use of the Gk. word eusebeia, “godliness,” in 2 Pet. 1:3, 6, 7; 3:11; and in Acts 3:12, a word occurring elsewhere in the NT only in the Pastoral Epistles). Furthermore, stylistic differences between 1 and 2 Peter can be easily explained by the apostle’s use of different amanuenses (secretaries) without having to resort to the unlikely conclusion that a different author wrote each letter.","The claim that 2 Peter is pseudonymous is weak. Genuine examples of pseudepigraphy (writings by authors who are pretending to be someone else of greater authority) in early Christian literature are almost all heretical, an indication that the device was used to commend works of questionable and false content. There is strong evidence that the church did not tolerate the practice, but in fact strictly rejected pseudepigrapha. The author introduces himself as “Simeon Peter,” rather than simply “Peter” (1 Pet. 1:1) or “Simon Peter,” the most frequent combination of his given name and the one bestowed on him by Jesus.
