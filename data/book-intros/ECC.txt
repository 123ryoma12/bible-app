But before looking at these themes, it is important to understand two foundational concepts: (1) “vanity” and (2) “under the sun.”","The Hb. word translated “vanity” (hebel) occurs thirty-eight times in the book of Ecclesiastes and is thus a key term for understanding the book. There is no single English term that captures the meaning of Hb. hebel. On the one hand, hebel refers to that which is frustrating. The frustration of life is articulated in the opening poem (1:3–11). The poem begins with a question, “What does a man gain by all his toil at which he toils under the sun?” The illustrations that follow answer the question with a resounding “nothing.” One labors to earn or accomplish something, not nothing; thus, to get nothing as a result of one’s labor is frustrating. The frustrating side of life is also articulated in the image of chasing after the wind, an image used frequently in the book (1:14, 17; 2:11, 17, 26; 4:4, 6, 16; 6:9; cf. 5:16). On the other hand, hebel also refers to that which is perplexing. The perplexing side of life is articulated in texts, such as 3:11; 7:14; and 8:17. There are experiences in life that humans simply cannot understand. Finally, the term hebel also carries the nuance of “fleeting.” A text such as 11:10 reminds us that youth is fleeting—here today and gone tomorrow.","The Hb. phrase translated “under the sun” occurs twenty-eight times in the book of Ecclesiastes and is thus also a key phrase for understanding the book. “Under the sun” refers to life in this world, as is clear from the fact that “under the sun” covers the period while one is alive (6:12) and before one dies (9:3–16). But there is more to it than that. “Under the sun” does not simply refer to life in this world but to life in a world that has been adversely affected by the fall. From this perspective, the book of Ecclesiastes can be seen as a biblical commentary on Genesis 3:16–19. In addition to the specific allusions to Gen. 3:16–19 found in Eccl. 3:20 and 12:7 with reference to humanity’s return to the dust, there is also the convergence of the term “vanity” and the phrase “under the sun” (1:14; 2:11, 17, 19; 4:7). “Under the sun” is the sphere where “vanity” is experienced. “Under the sun” is the place where humans experience the frustrating/perplexing side of life.","This brings us to our first theme, which is facing the frustrating/perplexing side of life. The book of Ecclesiastes will not allow a simplistic or unreasonably optimistic view of life. Knowing and trusting God does not automatically eliminate the frustrating/perplexing side of life. At times, in fact, knowing the Lord can seem to make life more perplexing (e.g., 6:1, 2). Furthermore, fearing God prevents us from making unfulfilled promises, pledges that can contribute to our feeling that life is empty because their fulfillment is empty (5:4–7). Rather, Ecclesiastes sets forth the teaching that with regard to our experience of life, God is absolutely sovereign and human beings are absolutely responsible.","A key text that focuses on divine sovereignty is 5:18–6:3. The ability to enjoy life is after all a gift from a sovereign God (2:24, 25).
