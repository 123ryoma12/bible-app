In the middle of the nineteenth century, however, Micah was not immune from the profoundly skeptical analyses of critical scholarship. Such scholarship identified as many as twenty-one distinct oracles within the book, which were assigned diverse origins—some as early as David; others as late as the first century B.C. In this scheme, only chapters 1–3 (with the exception of 2:12, 13) were assigned to the biblical prophet. More recent critical scholarship continues to see the book as the end product of an extensive process of editing and enlargement, though there is general acceptance that material original to Micah is contained in it, and there is greater interest in the unity of the book as a whole. In fact, more conservative scholars have pointed out that the unity of the work demonstrates that the book is from Micah himself and that much of the skepticism regarding Micah’s authorship is driven by ill-founded presuppositions about the impossibility of supernatural prophecy, not the actual evidence.
