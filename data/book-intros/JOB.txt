It provides another perspective to complement traditional wisdom as it wrestles with the suffering of a righteous individual alongside the affirmation that God is just and good. Sometimes called “retribution theology,” the simplistic version of this traditional or folk wisdom holds that good behavior is always rewarded and bad or immoral behavior is always punished, that the good succeed and the evil do not. Throughout the Wisdom Literature, we see the biblical authors wrestling with the temporal outworking of God’s justice and the question of suffering. Job, in particular, treats the subject with a frankness that is often baffling. The book does not suggest that there is an evil deity or that God’s power is limited. Instead it praises the Creator’s sovereignty, wisdom, and glory. That the God of Job is neither evil nor limited is seen in two portrayals of Job’s relationship with God.","The first is taken from the book’s prologue and epilogue. The prologue depicts Job’s submission to the divine will in the midst of suffering. There Job appears as a good man who trusts the goodness of his God. Finally, in the epilogue (42:7–17), God honors his trust by restoring him.","The second portrait in the poetic core of the book (chs. 3–42:6) shows Job’s indignation over his plight. He thinks that God has become his enemy and is unjustly afflicting him. Job comes to this conclusion because he knows that God is sovereign. If he believed in a limited deity, he would have no problem with God’s justice or goodness, since then he could not hold God responsible for every event, including his misfortune.","The superb literary artistry with which the material in the book is presented is universally acknowledged. Three main classifications have been proposed for the genre of Job: lawsuit, lament, and disputation. Lawsuit is supported by the use of legal language (e.g. 5:8; 13:3; 23:6) and procedures (e.g. ch. 32; 40:7, 8). This analysis focuses on Job as instituting proceedings against God. Initially an informal resolution is sought (chs. 4–14), and when that fails, more formal action is taken (chs. 15–31). After Job’s friends, who have acted as self-appointed advocates for God, fall silent and thereby concede the case, Elihu appeals against their decision (chs. 32–37). However, it takes direct divine intervention before Job withdraws his suit (40:5). Lament analyzes the book on the analogy of the lament psalms (see Introduction to Psalms: Genre). The laments found in chs. 3 and 29–31 support this approach. In Job the lament is presented in dramatic form with the friends’ intervention interpreted as extending comfort to the one in distress. Disputation uses the controversy dialogues recorded in ancient Near Eastern literature to elucidate the framework of Job, which obviously consists of a series of controversial interchanges between wisdom speakers. Their speeches are not primarily dialogue in which opponents’ viewpoints are taken up and answered. That may happen, but the disputation is closer to the presentation of a series of position papers to argue a particular case. However, while each of these interpretations reflects aspects of the book of Job, none provides an exhaustive analysis. Job is a unique and powerful literary creation precisely because it is a skillful blend of various genres. Verbal irony is a figure of speech in which the intended meaning is the opposite of the literal sense conveyed by the words used (e.g., 12:2). This is achieved in Job by the way in which the poetic dialogues are embedded within a prose framework. God cannot condone human sin, and Job and his counselors agree that the wicked will receive punishment from on high. At its simplest, the retribution principle states the scriptural truth that God punishes the wicked and rewards the faithful (see Prov. 11:21; Gal. 6:7, 9). But an orthodox retribution principle does not specify when, or how speedily, God will act. Moreover, it is to go beyond the teaching of Scripture to employ the retribution principle in reverse and make the simplistic conclusion that those who at present experience great misfortune or affliction are thereby proven to be great sinners (see Luke 13:1–5; John 9:1–3). This, however, is the logic that Job’s counselors employ: they witness his intense agony, and their theology leads them to infer that Job greatly sinned. These wisdom teachers overextend the applicability of the retribution principle so as to have a theology with no loose ends. For them, suffering is punishment or chastisement. They see no place for innocent suffering, which would attribute unworthiness to God for allowing it to happen. They are determined to defend God, and they do utter many high sentiments regarding Him. What they say, however, is dismissive and condescending as regards Job. They cannot (or will not) grasp the nature of his predicament. Moreover, their approach binds God to their understanding of the world and leaves no room for grace.","Against the conclusions of his friends, Job solemnly and repeatedly protests, but he can no more explain his situation than his friends can, for they all share the same basic presuppositions. Job never doubts the existence of God, and he remains convinced that God is just. But he cannot reconcile that with what was happening to him. Even so, Job differs from the counselors in that he has no doubts about his personal integrity. Consequently, he is led to question the adequacy of God’s administration of justice (see 9:21–24; 12:4–6; 24:12; 40:8).
