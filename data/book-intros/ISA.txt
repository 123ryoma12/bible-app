However, for the past two centuries, many higher-critical scholars have disputed the unity of Isaiah, arguing that the book is the result of a complex transmission through many hands. They generally divide the book into three sections, assigning these to Isaiah of Jerusalem (chs. 1–39); to a prophet living at the time of the exile (second, or “Deutero-Isaiah,” chs. 40–55); and to a postexilic prophet (the “Third Isaiah,” chs. 56–66).","One of the main evidences that these higher-critical scholars point to as evidence of at least three authors for the book of Isaiah are differences in language (e.g., different vocabulary) between the three sections of the work. However, while there are some linguistic differences between the three sections, the linguistic unity between them is often downplayed. In fact the use of the same terms throughout the three sections but almost nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible is key counterevidence to the claims of these scholars. Furthermore, any variations in language between the proposed divisions of the book are easily explainable as resulting from differences in subject matter, changed perspectives, or the prophet’s personal growth.","Nevertheless, despite their best attempts, the aforementioned scholars have been unable to completely ignore the similarities among the three sections. Instead of positing a single author, however, many of these critics have concluded that a final (late) editorial hand unified the work. Indeed, there are unifying features of the book of Isaiah that cannot be honestly overlooked. These include the repetition of theological themes (Zion, the coming of God, the Davidic representative, and the outworking of the Lord’s sovereign plan), the names for God (“the Holy One of Israel”; cf. 1:4 and note), and the unified message of judgment and salvation. Based on this reality and many other evidences, conservative scholars defend the traditional understanding that the book is all the work of one prophet, Isaiah. The book was published under his name (1:1), and he is frequently mentioned in it (2:1; 7:3; 13:1; etc.). The NT names Isaiah as the author of passages from all parts of the book (Matt. 3:3; 8:17; 12:17; note esp. the combined references in John 12:37–41).","Another reason why certain higher-critical scholars question Isaiah’s unity of authorship is their assumption that a prophet addresses only his contemporaries. This comes from their belief that only Isaiah’s contemporaries would have found his message relevant, and so Isaiah would have only spoken of things that immediately concerned them. They argue that since chapters 1–39 are addressed to the nation during the Assyrian invasions (c. 740–700 B.C.), chapters 40–55 to the exiles in Babylon (c. 600–539 B.C.), and chapters 56–66 to the community after its return to the land (c. 539–500 B.C.), more than one prophet must have spoken, each making a fresh application of the themes of Isaiah of Jerusalem to his own generation. But this ignores that prophets often ask one generation to participate by faith in a salvation that lies in the distant future. Even though many people were unfaithful during Isaiah’s own day, a core group of faithful disciples (8:16–18) needed the encouragement of knowing that God’s promises to Israel were still in effect and that after the coming darkness of which Isaiah spoke, light would dawn (9:2–7). Furthermore, Moses anticipated the inevitability of Israel’s exile and return (Deut. 30:1–3), as did Solomon (1 Kin. 8:46–51). It is not far-fetched to imagine that, under the Lord’s inspiration, Isaiah also addressed similar future concerns, which had vital immediate ramifications in summoning the people of his own day to repentance and faith in the Lord who governs their future.","Finally, a distinct anti-supernaturalistic bias on the part of many higher-critical scholars explains why many of them divide the book of Isaiah among at least three different authors. Critics often assume that miraculous prophecy, such as naming Cyrus more than a century beforehand and predicting the birth of Jesus from the Virgin Mary, is impossible. This unargued-for presupposition then drives their conclusions that the prophecy of Cyrus must have been written after his appearance and that Isa. 7 has nothing to do with the birth of Jesus. However, Isaiah reproaches Ahaz for precisely this kind of unbelief when the king refuses to recognize the Lord’s ability to demonstrate His power through signs (7:10–13). The Lord gave Hezekiah just such a sign as an assurance of his recovery (38:7, 8). In chapters 40–55, the Lord validates His sovereign rule over the nations precisely by the power of His prophets to speak the future before it happens. Pagan prophets could not show that their past prophecies had been fulfilled, nor could they give reliable prophecies of what was to come (41:21–29). By contrast, Israel’s God fulfilled Isaiah’s former prophecies about Assyria. The prophet also predicts the future, mentioning Cyrus by name. Isaiah delivers these supernatural prophecies (41:21–29; 44:24–45:8) to leave unbelievers without excuse (48:3–5).
