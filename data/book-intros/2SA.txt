The book may be divided into four sections in regard to his kingship: David’s ascendancy to the throne, first over Judah and then over all Israel (1:1–5:5); David’s glorious kingship (5:6–10:19); the failure of his kingship (11:1–20:26); and then some final reflections on his kingship (21:1–24:25), including his last words (23:1–7).","In addition to the themes covered in the introduction to 1 Samuel, additional features of 2 Samuel require highlighting. Importantly, 2 Sam. 7 describes the covenant that God established with David. Although God denied David’s request to build a temple for him (vv. 4–7), He promised, through the prophet Nathan, that one of David’s “offspring” would be established on the throne of Israel (v. 12). This descendant would one day build a house, or temple, for the Lord (v. 13a). Additionally, the eternal nature of the descendant’s kingship is underscored as God vowed that it would last “forever” (vv. 13b, 16). God would discipline David’s offspring for iniquity but would not abandon him (vv. 15–16). This prophecy found its initial fulfillment in David’s son Solomon: Solomon was David’s direct descendant, he was established as king over Israel, and he built the temple of the Lord. The prophecy of the Davidic covenant, however, has greater expectations than what occurred in the time of Solomon, especially in regard to the kingship’s eternality. Indeed, there appeared a greater fulfillment of the prophecy in the coming of Jesus Christ: He is the son of David, He is the true king of Israel, He is building a greater temple (Heb. 3:1–4), and His throne is eternal.","The pivot of the book of 2 Samuel is David’s sin with Bathsheba and against Uriah in chapters 11–12. When confronted by Nathan, David’s repentance was genuine, and God’s forgiveness was immediate. Yet sin still had its consequences for David and his reign. In his later years, David witnessed his own sins replicated in the lives of his sons. God further promised that “the sword shall never depart” from David’s house (12:10). The king lived to see four of his sons die, three of them—Absalom, Amnon, and Adonijah—by the sword. Not until David survived two rebellions, first by his son Absalom and then by Sheba, son of Bichri (ch. 20), did David’s reign regain a measure of equilibrium.","Thematic closure is provided for the books of 1 and 2 Samuel by a kind of epilogue in 2 Sam. 21–24. These chapters are symmetrical in outline, and at the center are two poems celebrating the two fundamental reasons for David’s blessedness. First, the Lord was his deliverer (22:2–51); and, second, the Lord made an “everlasting covenant” with him (23:1–7).
