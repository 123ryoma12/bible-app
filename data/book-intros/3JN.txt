The letter’s recipient, Gaius, belonged to the blessed company of those who became “fellow workers for [God’s] truth” (v. 8) by housing, feeding, and funding the bearers of God’s Word. This company includes a Gentile widow in Zarephath who fed the prophet Elijah at the risk, she expected, of her own and her son’s starvation (1 Kin. 17:8–16), and the woman of Shunem who persuaded her husband to remodel their home to accommodate the visits of the prophet Elisha (2 Kin. 4:8–10). Obadiah risked his life and proved true to his name (“Yahweh’s servant”) by hiding a hundred true prophets in a cave and bringing them bread and water (1 Kin. 18:12, 13). When Jesus sent out the Twelve and the seventy-two, He promised that as they traveled to spread the good news of God’s kingdom, they would be provided for by people of peace (Luke 9:3–5; 10:4–9). In receiving Jesus’ messengers, these hosts were welcoming Jesus Himself, so they would share in the messengers’ reward (Matt. 10:40–42). In Philippi, Lydia’s first instinct upon coming to faith was to open her home to welcome the missionary team of Paul and Silas (Acts 16:15). When Paul wrote his letter to the Romans, he expected to be “helped on my journey” (same Gk. verb as in 3 John 7) to Spain by their financial support after a spiritually refreshing sojourn among them (Rom. 15:24). Hebrews urges its hearers not to neglect “hospitality to strangers,” subtly reminding them that when Abraham welcomed unknown guests he was entertaining angels (Heb. 13:2, alluding to Gen. 18:1–15).","Such assistance not only shows love for one’s fellow believers, but it will also be repaid by the Lord Himself.
